03

And why meat?

From Homo habilis, through Homo erectus and Homo neanderthalensis, to Homo sapiens

Meat was our main food

Why?

Eating meat maximizes the energy we get and minimizes the time we spend feeding

Optimal Foraging Theory describes our preference for the foods that give the greatest possible return in calories.

The most nutrient-dense, calorie-rich food is meat, complete in vitamins, minerals, fatty acids and essential amino acids.

We developed a larger brain, balanced by a smaller, simpler gastrointestinal tract, which needs high-quality food based on the protein and fat of meat.

Where did we go wrong?

By focusing on the wrong food groups.

We came to base our diet on carbohydrates

such as bread, pasta and grains

But there is no such thing as an essential carbohydrate: it is an entirely optional nutrient

Through gluconeogenesis, the liver can make 200 g of glucose a day, more than the 120 g the brain needs.

When we switch to fat as our energy source, we produce ketone bodies, the preferred fuel of neurons.

05

Why is meat always cast as the villain?

Epidemiological studies

Many studies compare people who eat more meat with people who eat little or no meat. These comparisons in observational studies are full of confounding factors that the statistical analyses in these papers do not adequately resolve.

They are useful for raising hypotheses, but they do not establish cause and effect; they establish a correlation.

When epidemiological or observational studies implicate red meat in causing disease, they compare, within a pool of thousands of people answering questionnaires, those who eat less red meat with those who eat more.

Could these groups differ in other ways, besides the kind of meat they eat?

The healthy user bias

People who follow health advice to the letter, such as eating less red meat, tend to look after themselves in every other way too. So, in an observational study, meat ends up taking the blame for other habits.

Inuit

The Inuit diet

Members of the Eskimo Indigenous nation who live in the Arctic of Canada, Alaska and Greenland. They ate the meat of seals, reindeer, birds, walruses, bears, fish and whales.

It is remarkable how well they do on a diet high in fat and protein. The traditional Eskimo diet is practically free of vegetables, crops or dairy, and quite low in carbohydrates.

They did not develop scurvy or any disease caused by vitamin C deficiency, and they showed that today’s nutritional theory of a “balanced diet” is nonsense.

North American Indians

Known as some of the tallest people in the world, with a strong build, Native American Indians hunted large animals such as bison, deer, buffalo, wild sheep, goats, antelope, elk, caribou and bears.

The Maasai diet

The Maasai tribes are semi-nomadic groups who live mainly in Tanzania and Kenya. Their life revolves around cattle, the basis of their diet: meat, milk and blood.

In the 1960s, the physician George V. Mann studied them in Kenya. They ate only meat, blood and milk, and he noted that they regarded fruit and vegetables as food for cows.

Were there other tribes?

  • The Chukchi of the Russian Arctic lived on caribou meat, sea animals and fish.
  • The Samburu and Rendille warriors of East Africa survived on diets made up mainly of milk and meat.
  • Mongolian nomads ate meat in winter, and meat and dairy in summer.
  • The Sioux of South Dakota enjoyed a diet of buffalo meat.

What was their health like?

Dentist and researcher

Weston Price studied and documented the health of primitive peoples

He was a dentist and one of the greatest contributors to what we know today about how diet affects our physical and mental health.

For years he sought out peoples who lived far from civilization yet enjoyed full health.

He studied more than 14 cultures around the world, across Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Pacific.

+14cultures studied

4regions: Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Pacific

Findings of Weston A. Price

  1. None of the traditional diets contained refined products such as sugar, fructose syrups, white flour, canned food, pasteurized, skimmed or semi-skimmed dairy, hydrogenated vegetable oils, artificial vitamins and toxic additives.
  2. All traditional cultures ate animal protein and fat, including fish and shellfish, poultry, beef, eggs, milk and dairy, reptiles and insects.
  3. All of them ate animal bones, usually as mineral-rich broths.
  4. All of them ate healthy, saturated fats of animal origin.
  5. All of them ate animal products and always used the whole animal, including organs and offal. Some kinds of animal products were eaten raw.

Vilhjalmur Stefansson

Explorer and ethnologist of the American Arctic, of Icelandic descent

He explored the Arctic and, instead of carrying large food supplies, he ate what the Inuit ate: fish, walrus and other meats.

This was in the early 20th century, when nutrition experts recommended raw vegetables and as little meat as possible.

Stefansson documented the Inuit diet, but met with skepticism from people who did not believe him.

1928 · Bellevue Hospital, New York

Experiment: a year of carnivore

Stefansson was not trying to burn fat, but to prove that the Inuit’s meat-rich diet was viable.

To prove it, Stefansson and a fellow explorer followed an all-meat diet in 1928 for a whole year. They began the experiment in a hospital, where doctors could monitor their health.

The doctors who examined the two men during the year-long study reported that neither of them had any problems on a carnivore diet.

“There were no deficiency problems; both men stayed perfectly healthy; their bowels stayed normal; their stools were smaller and odorless.”

1929

The effect of an exclusive meat diet lasting one year on the carbohydrate tolerance of two normal men

Edward Tolstoi · Russell Sage Institute of Pathology and Bellevue Hospital, New York

Journal of Biological Chemistry 83(3):747–752

doi:10.1016/S0021-9258(18)77101-9

01

Is there a difference between plant and animal proteins?

  • Most plant foods lack at least 1 of the 9 essential amino acids.
  • Plant proteins are more likely to cause allergies and food sensitivities.
  • The main allergens are plant proteins: gluten, soy, corn, tree nuts and peanuts.
  • Plant proteins are less bioavailable than animal proteins.

02

What about vitamin C?

  • There is vitamin C in meat, fish roe, liver, kidney and pancreas.
  • Do you drink orange juice to get vitamin C? Glucose and vitamin C are almost identical, and all that sugar will block the absorption of vitamin C.
  • Eating fewer carbohydrates lowers your need for vitamin C.
  • If you eat a little fresh meat every day and don’t overcook it, you will get enough vitamin C to prevent scurvy.

03

Is fiber really necessary?

  • Fiber is not essential, since fiber is a carbohydrate.
  • Fiber is not a nutrient: it is not absorbed and does not nourish.
  • There is no evidence that fiber protects against heart disease, colon cancer, irritable bowel syndrome, intestinal polyps, chronic constipation or hemorrhoids.
  • Contrary to popular belief, a high intake of dietary fiber is not necessary for healthy bowel movements.

2012

Conclusion: idiopathic constipation and its associated symptoms can be effectively reduced by stopping, or even just reducing, dietary fiber intake.

Stopping or reducing dietary fiber intake reduces constipation and its associated symptoms

Kok-Sun Ho, Charmaine You Mei Tan, Muhd Ashik Mohd Daud, Francis Seow-Choen

World Journal of Gastroenterology 18(33):4593–4596

doi:10.3748/wjg.v18.i33.4593

04

What about cholesterol?

  • Every animal cell needs cholesterol for proper structure and function.
  • It is essential for producing hormones and neurotransmitters, and crucial for producing vitamin D.
  • It protects against cognitive decline.
  • The cholesterol we eat has little or no influence on the cholesterol levels in our blood.

05

Isn’t eating fat harmful?

  • Certain essential vitamins, such as A, D, E and K, need fat to be absorbed.
  • It is the best source of energy for human metabolism.
  • It regulates our appetite and helps us feel full.
  • Saturated fat is associated with longevity, better cognitive function, a stronger immune system and hormone production, and it raises HDL.

2010

There is no significant evidence to conclude that saturated fat is associated with a higher risk of coronary or cardiovascular disease.

Meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies evaluating the association of saturated fat with cardiovascular disease

Patty W Siri-Tarino, Qi Sun, Frank B Hu, Ronald M Krauss

The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition 91(3):535–546

doi:10.3945/ajcn.2009.27725

06

1953

Meat and heart disease: data manipulation

  • He manipulated data to conclude that animal fats were causing heart disease.
  • He believed that eating fat raised blood cholesterol.
  • He published his research showing 6 countries with a high intake of animal fat and their higher associated risk of death from heart disease.
  • He left another 16 countries out of the study: the ones that did not support his hypothesis.

Yudkin removed the stigma from saturated fat

Yudkin argued that saturated fat is closely tied to our evolution: it is abundant, for example, in breast milk.

In his view, we were more likely to fall ill not from the prehistoric substance but from the recent innovation: sugar.

07

But doesn’t meat cause cancer?

  • Obese people have higher rates of cancer.
  • Any factor that predisposes to obesity is a risk factor for cancer. This means that eating too many carbohydrates increases the risk.
  • There is no solid scientific evidence to support the belief that red meat increases the risk of cancer.

Vilhjalmur Stefansson, Weston A. Price and Thomas L. Cleave all agree in their observations:

Cancer was a non-existent disease in societies with a traditional lifestyle

08

Meat and kidneys

  • The claim that dietary protein causes kidney disease is not supported by evidence.
  • Diabetes, especially type 2, is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease and end-stage kidney disease worldwide, and hypertension is the second (both are hyperinsulinemic conditions).
  • Refined, high-glycemic carbohydrates can endanger the human kidney, not animal protein.
  • Keep your kidneys healthy by controlling your blood sugar and blood pressure.

Logical fallacy

  • When the kidneys are severely damaged, they may be unable to metabolize larger amounts of protein, but that does not mean protein is the cause of the problem.
  • Nobody ends up on dialysis from eating too much steak and eggs.

1930

Kidney function tests revealed no evidence of kidney damage.

Clinical calorimetry XLV. Prolonged meat diets with a study of kidney function and ketosis

Walter S. McClellan, Eugene F. Du Bois

Journal of Biological Chemistry 87(3):651–668

doi:10.1016/S0021-9258(18)76842-7

2018

A systematic review of 28 randomized controlled trials found no difference in glomerular filtration rate between people on a high-protein diet (more than 1.5 g/kg/day) and people on a normal- or low-protein diet.

Changes in kidney function do not differ between healthy adults consuming higher- compared with lower- or normal-protein diets: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Michaela C Devries, Arjun Sithamparapillai, K Scott Brimble, Laura Banfield, Robert W Morton, Stuart M Phillips

The Journal of Nutrition 148(11):1760–1775

doi:10.1093/jn/nxy197

09

Meat and the heart

  • We have been eating animal meat for two million years, but heart disease has only been a major problem for us for about 50 years.
  • Refined carbohydrates, such as sugar and flour, and vegetable oils are the most important dietary risk factors for coronary artery disease.
  • Adding significant amounts of refined carbohydrates is responsible for most “diseases of civilization”: diabetes, obesity and heart disease.

1964

Despite a long, continuous diet of only meat and milk, the men have low serum cholesterol levels and no evidence of atherosclerotic heart disease. This supports the conclusion that dietary fat is not responsible for coronary disease.

Cardiovascular disease in the Masai

G.V. Mann, R.D. Shaffer, R.S. Anderson, H.H. Sandstead and others

Journal of Atherosclerosis Research 4(4):289–312

doi:10.1016/S0368-1319(64)80041-7

10

Meat and gout

  • It is not meat but chronically high insulin that causes gout: high insulin raises uric acid, and high uric acid often leads to gout.
  • Drinking alcohol can contribute to gout, and seems to do so in a dose-dependent way.
  • A growing body of evidence suggests it may also be linked to consuming large amounts of fructose.
